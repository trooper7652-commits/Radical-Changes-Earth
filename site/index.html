Eleven years of Earth at night, played as music.

The important thing isn’t can you read music, it’s can you hear it. Can you hear the music, Robert?

Niels Bohr, as portrayed in Oppenheimer (2023)

We measured seven frames of NASA’s night-lights map: 2012, 2017, 2022, 2023 and three maps of the change. Each measurement drives one rule of the music. More light pulls the song toward a minor key, and bigger changes between years bring in the hang drum, balafon and string bass, louder and higher. You don’t have to see the map to hear it change.

    Instruments, one for each voice

    Six rules, measured from the pixels.

    1. Melody and key

      The main color is compared with the average color of each column, left to right. That sets the melody’s pitch and the key within the list of keys (key order from Hooktheory, “Key popularity”).

    2. Tempo

      Vibrancy is averaged 50/50 with the size of change from the previous frame.

    3. Major or minor

      Happiness is 100 minus the share of pixels at brightness 127 or more, stretched across the seven frames to 1 to 100. More light means less happy. 50 or more gives a major key; below 50, a minor key.

    4. Short or long notes

      Uniqueness compares a bright spot with the light around it. It sets short or long notes. Every note rings 1 beat past the next note of its voice, and each frame’s last notes ring 1 beat into the next frame, so the music never pauses.

    5. Instrument family

      Contrast compares connected full-brightness pixels with dark pixels. High contrast gives synth and piano. Low contrast with short notes gives harp and bells, and the handpan joins the harp. Low contrast with long notes gives a low synth and a handpan playing the same notes, the synth one octave below the handpan, over sustain strings.

    6. Change voices

      The size of change from the previous frame, doubled and capped at 100, drives the hang drum, balafon and string bass: the bigger the change, the louder, busier and higher they play. They are silent in the first frame. The oboe doubles every melody note.

      Built for listening.

      The Earth Information Jukebox challenge asks how NASA’s Earth Information Center visualizations can reach people who can’t see them. Our answer keeps the map’s numbers and gives them to the ear: every note on this page comes from a measurement of a real NASA frame.
